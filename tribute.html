Her Story

The Enchantress of Numbers

How the daughter of a poet became the person who first described what a computer could do.

Augusta Ada King, later Countess of Lovelace, was the only child of the poet Lord Byron, though she never really knew him — her parents separated within weeks of her birth, and her father left England for good before she turned one. Her mother, wary of anything resembling her father's temperament, pushed Ada toward mathematics and logic from an early age, hoping rigor would keep her steady. It worked, though not in the way her mother expected: Ada grew into a thinker who refused to separate precision from imagination, calling her own approach "poetical science."

At seventeen, Ada was introduced to the mathematician and inventor Charles Babbage, who had spent years designing mechanical calculating devices. She was captivated by his unbuilt Analytical Engine, a proposed machine far more ambitious than any calculator before it. Where most of Babbage's contemporaries saw only a faster way to crunch numbers, Ada saw something else entirely: a machine that could, in principle, manipulate any information that could be expressed in symbols — not just numbers, but music, language, and pattern.

In 1843, Ada translated an Italian engineer's paper on the Analytical Engine into English, and then went far beyond translation. She added a set of her own notes, nearly three times the length of the original text, laying out in careful detail how the machine could be instructed to calculate a complex sequence known as Bernoulli numbers. That step-by-step method is now widely regarded as the first published computer program, written more than a century before the first electronic computers existed.

Ada died of cancer in 1852 at just thirty-six, having never seen a machine like the one she wrote for actually built. It would take another hundred years for computing to catch up to what she had already imagined: a device that worked not just with quantity, but with meaning itself.

A Life, In Order

Key moments

The events, in sequence, that shaped how Ada Lovelace came to see the future of computing before it existed.

1815

Born in London

Born to Lord Byron and Anne Isabella Milbanke. Her parents separated a month later, and she never formed a relationship with her father.

1833

Meets Charles Babbage

At seventeen, Ada was introduced to Babbage through her tutor, mathematician Mary Somerville, and first saw a prototype of his difference engine.

1835

Marries William King

She married William King, later the Earl of Lovelace, becoming the Countess of Lovelace. They had three children together.

1843

Publishes her Notes

Her translation and original notes on the Analytical Engine were published, including a detailed method for computing Bernoulli numbers — now considered the first computer program.

1852

Dies at thirty-six

Ada died of cervical cancer and was buried beside the father she barely knew, at her own request.

1980

A language named Ada

The U.S. Department of Defense named its newly standardized programming language "Ada" in her honor.

The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.

Why She Still Matters

Remembered in code and in stone

Ada's name now sits on a programming language, a GPU architecture, and an annual day honoring women in STEM.

Ada, the Language

A programming language built for the U.S. Department of Defense was named Ada in 1980, its reference manual numbered 1815 after her birth year.

Ada Lovelace Day

Held every October since 2009, this annual event celebrates women in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics worldwide.

Statues & Silicon

Beyond a 2022 London statue, her name marks an Nvidia GPU architecture and a 2023 Royal Mint commemorative coin.
